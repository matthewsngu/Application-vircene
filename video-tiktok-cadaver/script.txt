You think death gets you out of court. Wrong.
In 897, a pope put a nine-month-old corpse on trial. And found it guilty.
This is the Cadaver Synod.
Rome, January 897. Pope Stephen the Sixth wants revenge on a former pope, Formosus. So he has him dug up from his grave and carried into the Lateran basilica.
The corpse gets three things: papal robes, a throne, and a deacon to speak for him.
So how does the trial work?
Stephen screams the charges at the body. The main crime: becoming pope while he was bishop of another city, against church law. The deacon tries to answer for him. The dead man says nothing. Nine months in the grave: about two hundred seventy days. Verdict: guilty.
They cut off the three fingers he blessed people with, rip off his robes, and throw his body into the Tiber River. Rome is horrified.
And here's the twist: months later, Stephen himself is thrown in prison and strangled. And the next popes ban putting the dead on trial.
So who was really on trial that day?
