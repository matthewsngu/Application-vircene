You think a scam needs a genius. Wrong.
In 1920, one man promised fifty percent profit in forty-five days. Forty thousand people believed him.
His name was Charles Ponzi.
Boston, 1919. Ponzi is broke. Then he finds a little postal coupon you could trade for stamps in another country. Cheaper in Italy, worth more in America.
His pitch had three parts: a real coupon, a simple math trick, and a promise of fast money.
So how did it work?
There was no real profit. Buying that many coupons was impossible. Instead, Ponzi paid early investors with money from new investors. They got rich, told their friends, and even more money poured in. At its peak, he took in a million dollars a week. The counter climbs: fifteen million dollars.
August 1920. A newspaper investigates. Investors rush to get their money back. The money is gone. Six banks collapse.
And here's the twist: Ponzi died poor in Brazil with seventy-five dollars. But his name lives on. Today, every scam like his is called a Ponzi scheme.
So be honest: would you have invested?
