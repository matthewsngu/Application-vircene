You think a sweet syrup could never kill anyone. Wrong.
In 1919, a wave of molasses swept through Boston and killed twenty-one people.
This is the Great Molasses Flood.
Boston's North End, January 1919. Next to the harbor stands a giant steel tank, fifty feet tall, full of molasses for making alcohol. People walk past it every day.
The tank had three problems: walls too thin, seams that leaked, and it had never been properly tested. The company even painted it brown to hide the leaks.
So how does syrup kill?
At lunchtime, the tank bursts open. Molasses is heavier than water, so the wave hits at thirty-five miles an hour, up to twenty-five feet high. It crushes buildings and bends the steel of an elevated railway. The counter climbs: two million three hundred thousand gallons. Then the cold January air thickens it. People get stuck like flies in honey.
Twenty-one people die. About a hundred fifty are hurt. The harbor stays brown for months.
And here's the twist: the company blamed anarchists. Six years later, a court ruled the tank was simply unsafe.
So what giant tank is standing near you right now?
